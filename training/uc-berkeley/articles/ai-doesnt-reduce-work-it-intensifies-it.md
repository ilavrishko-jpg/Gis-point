# AI Doesn't Reduce Work — It Intensifies It

> Source: `AI Doesn't Reduce Work—It Intensifies It.pdf`
> Harvard Business Review, February 9, 2026 — by Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye
> Original: https://hbr.org/2026/02/ai-doesnt-reduce-work-it-intensifies-it
> *(Referenced in Caroline Simard's session as the "AI brain fry" research from a Berkeley scholar.)*

---

## Summary
One promise of AI is that it reduces workloads so employees can focus on higher-value, more engaging tasks. But new research finds AI tools **don't reduce work — they consistently intensify it.** In an eight-month study of generative AI at a ~200-employee US technology company, employees worked at a faster pace, took on a broader scope of tasks, and extended work into more hours of the day — often without being asked. The company did not mandate AI use (though it offered enterprise subscriptions). Workers did more on their own initiative because AI made "doing more" feel possible, accessible, and intrinsically rewarding.

This can be unsustainable. Once the excitement of experimenting fades, workload has quietly grown; workers feel stretched. That **workload creep** leads to cognitive fatigue, burnout, and weakened decision-making. The early productivity surge can give way to lower-quality work and turnover. Asking employees to self-regulate isn't a winning strategy — companies need norms and standards around AI use: an **"AI practice."**

---

## How Generative AI Intensifies Work
The study ran April-December (in-person observation two days/week, tracking internal communication channels, 40+ in-depth interviews across engineering, product, design, research, operations). Three forms of intensification:

### 1. Task Expansion
Because AI fills knowledge gaps, workers stepped into responsibilities that previously belonged to others — PMs and designers writing code, researchers taking on engineering, individuals attempting work they'd have outsourced, deferred, or avoided. AI provided an empowering cognitive boost (reduced dependence on others, immediate feedback). Workers described "just trying things," but these experiments accumulated into a meaningful widening of job scope — absorbing work that might previously have justified additional headcount.

**Knock-on effects:** engineers spent more time reviewing, correcting, and guiding AI-generated work by colleagues — coaching "vibe-coding" colleagues and finishing partially complete pull requests, often informally in Slack threads or desk-side consultations.

### 2. Blurred Boundaries Between Work and Non-Work
Because AI made starting a task easy (reducing the friction of the blank page), workers slipped small amounts of work into former breaks — prompting AI during lunch, in meetings, or while waiting for a file to load; sending a "quick last prompt" before stepping away. These rarely felt like more work, yet produced a workday with fewer natural pauses. The conversational style softened the experience (typing to an AI felt like chatting), making work spill into evenings and early mornings. Downtime no longer provided the same recovery — work felt less bounded and more **ambient**, something that could always be advanced a little further.

### 3. More Multitasking
AI introduced a rhythm of managing several active threads at once — manually writing code while AI generated an alternative, running multiple agents in parallel, reviving long-deferred tasks. The sense of having a "partner" enabled momentum, but the reality was continual attention-switching, frequent checking of AI outputs, and a growing number of open tasks — cognitive load and constant juggling, even as work felt productive. Over time this raised expectations for speed (not through explicit demands, but through what became normalized).

## What This Means for Organizations — and How an "AI Practice" Can Help
A self-reinforcing cycle: AI accelerated tasks → raised speed expectations → increased reliance on AI → widened scope → expanded quantity and density of work. Many felt more productive but not less busy — sometimes busier. As one engineer put it: *"You had thought that maybe, because you could be more productive with AI, you could work less. But really, you don't work less. You just work the same amount or even more."*

Organizations might see voluntary work expansion as a win — but what looks like higher productivity can mask silent workload creep and growing cognitive strain. Because the extra effort is voluntary and framed as enjoyable experimentation, leaders overlook the added load. Over time, overwork impairs judgment, increases errors, and makes it hard to distinguish genuine productivity gains from unsustainable intensity. For workers: fatigue, burnout, and a sense that work is harder to step away from.

Rather than responding passively, individuals and companies should adopt an **"AI practice"** — intentional norms and routines that structure how AI is used, when to stop, and how work should and shouldn't expand. Three components:

### Intentional Pauses
Brief, structured moments that regulate tempo — protected intervals to assess alignment, reconsider assumptions, or absorb information. They don't slow work overall; they prevent quiet overload accumulation. Example: a **decision pause** requiring, before a major decision is finalized, one counterargument and one explicit link to organizational goals.

### Sequencing
Norms that shape *when* work moves forward, not just how fast — batching non-urgent notifications, holding updates until natural breakpoints, protecting focus windows. Work advances in coherent phases rather than reacting to every AI output — less fragmentation, fewer costly context switches, preserved attention.

### Human Grounding
As AI enables more solo, self-contained work, protect time for listening and human connection — brief check-ins, shared reflection, structured dialogue. Beyond perspective, social exchange supports creativity: AI provides a single synthesized perspective, but creative insight depends on exposure to multiple human viewpoints. Institutionalizing time for dialogue re-anchors work in social context and counters the depleting, individualizing effects of fast, AI-mediated work.

---

## Closing
The promise of generative AI lies not only in what it can do for work, but in how thoughtfully it's integrated into the daily rhythm. Without intention, AI makes it easier to do more — but harder to stop. An AI practice preserves moments for recovery and reflection even as work accelerates. The question is not whether AI will change work, but whether organizations will actively shape that change — or let it quietly shape them.
