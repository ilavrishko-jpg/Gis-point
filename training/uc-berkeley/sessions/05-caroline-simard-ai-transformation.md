# Session Summary: Caroline Simard
## AI Transformation, Organizational Change, and Building AI-Native Startups

> Source: `2026-05-12-Carolyn Simard.pdf` — Berkeley AgriTech Accelerator

---

## 1. One-Page Executive Summary for Ukrainian Agritech Startups

### The Core Message
What happens *after* a company decides to "use AI." **AI transformation is not primarily a tool problem — it is an organizational change problem.** Adopting ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini won't automatically create productivity gains. If you layer AI on top of messy workflows, fragmented data, unclear roles, and cultural resistance, AI simply amplifies the dysfunction. The real work: map how work actually happens, understand where value is created, decide where AI should be centralized or decentralized, and build a culture where people can experiment safely.

For Ukrainian startups this is especially relevant — they're small enough to **leapfrog the AI maturity curve.** Large companies struggle with legacy systems, bureaucracy, old roles, siloed data, compliance fears, and slow alignment. Startups can design AI-native workflows from the beginning.

### What Startups Should Remember
1. **AI amplifies what already exists.** Good data + clear workflows + strong learning culture + fast decisions → AI accelerates growth. Broken processes + hidden bottlenecks + bias + fear + bureaucracy → AI scales those problems too.
2. **AI adoption has a J-curve.** Productivity may dip as teams learn, redesign workflows, and rethink roles. CFOs ask "where's the ROI?" too early — ROI comes when companies move beyond pilots and redesign how work is done.
3. **Don't automate the soul of the work.** Some work carries identity, craft, trust, and human value (luxury fashion; Grammarly's controversial "Stephen King editor"). Automating the wrong thing alienates employees, customers, creators, and partners.

### What Startups Should Apply
Start by **mapping workflows** — what's repetitive, painful, low-value, frustrating vs. what creates trust and differentiation. First AI use cases should target boring work everyone hates (paperwork, forms, reporting, repetitive analysis, internal docs). **"Boring is beautiful."** Decide when to **centralize** (high-risk use cases affecting people, safety, customers, food supply, health, legal) vs. **decentralize** (where speed and learning matter). Build **psychological safety** so teams can say "this failed," "I'm concerned," "this is risky," "I need help."

### What Should Inspire Them
**Innovation under constraint can be an advantage.** Operating under pressure, scarcity, and uncertainty forces clarity — knowing what matters, what creates value, what's essential. Combined with AI-native thinking, that becomes a competitive edge. The future startup may measure scale not by headcount but by how intelligently its systems learn, how clearly decisions are documented, how well workflows are mapped, and how effectively humans supervise AI agents.

## 2. Refined Transcript

### Introduction: Technology, Change, and Silicon Valley
Caroline is a social scientist studying organizational behavior, diversity, teams, innovation, and tech ecosystems. Now dean of Northeastern University in Silicon Valley (Northeastern is Boston-based, ~10 years in SV, 13 North American campuses + London). Many universities want an SV presence (Yale is moving to San Francisco). AI is forcing organizations to rethink how they work, do business, and operate.

### The Evolution of AI and the Productivity J-Curve
Compared the AI moment to the internet boom (she moved to SV in 1998 during the dot-com boom; the crash came, but the internet still changed business permanently). At Stanford 2000-2004, ML friends were told the field was too theoretical — now those skills are in fierce demand. Current phase: generative AI, especially language models; next: video, robotics, embodied/physical AI. The **productivity J-curve**: with transformative technologies (electricity, internet, AI), productivity often dips before it rises — organizations must change how they work first. Task-level gains are documented; firm-level ROI takes longer (redesign of workflows and business models). Recommended: **Erik Brynjolfsson** and **Stanford's Digital Economy Lab.**

### AI Maturity: Where Are Organizations Today?
Five-level model: (1) **Awareness** — early interest/tinkering; (2) **Active experimentation** — with tools and data; (3) **Operational** — AI embedded in processes producing value; (4) **Systemic** — AI supports broader transformation (supply chains); (5) **Transformational** — AI is part of the org's DNA. Most participants were at levels 1-2. Even her university, with early enterprise cloud AI (Claude), still runs on PDFs, forms, and bureaucracy — **tool access ≠ transformation.**

### Exercise: Barriers and Bright Spots
Reflect on barriers *and* bright spots. Leaders over-index on breaking down barriers and forget to amplify what's already working.

**Participant reflections:**
- *Resistance, strategy, roles, sales AI:* barriers = team skepticism, building a systematic approach, identifying new roles. Bright spots = efficiency, fewer mistakes, faster analysis. Use case: AI analyzes thousands of weekly sales-call transcripts vs. a script and gives automatic feedback — impossible without AI.
- *Predictive AI and the data barrier:* a university spin-off using predictive AI (marketing, forecasting) — main barrier is **data** (collecting raw data, right format).
- *Internal AI vs. product AI:* moved from skepticism to Claude for development + AI in marketing; realized employees need education. For product AI (document analysis, metadata extraction, a built-in agent), the barrier was ROI uncertainty (operating cost, pricing, willingness to pay). **Timing is everything** — right idea, wrong timing misses the window; waiting too long lets faster startups enter first.
- *AI as a requirement:* a company where all workers use AI (paid for a year); if employees don't use AI, their role is questioned. Almost no longer uses lawyers (chat-based tools); greenhouse workers teach robots to identify vegetables; predicts many roles replaced by robots within two years. Making AI a requirement accelerates adoption but can create resistance if mismanaged.

### Ukrainian Startups and Innovation Under Constraint
Ukrainian founders operate in an extremely difficult environment — building companies while supporting a country at war. This creates an **innovation-under-constraint mindset**: hyper-focused, clear about value because the stakes are high (sometimes life and death, part of a national mission). Despite the war, Ukraine's startup ecosystem keeps growing. Large SV companies are afraid because they can't change fast (legacy systems, processes, vendors); startups move faster and can leapfrog AI maturity stages.

### Lesson 1: AI Does Not Fix Broken Systems
The problem isn't tool access — it's **mapping where AI creates the most value**, understanding the business, workflows, and whether the structure must change. AI on a broken system amplifies brokenness. A Stanford lab study of 53 AI implementations found **broken workflows** the biggest problem. Companies think a process document = their workflow, but real work often depends on informal knowledge (e.g. "Amy" who knows who to call) — if Amy leaves, the documented process fails. Don't let IT launch tools without consulting business leaders, employees, marketing, HR, ops. Smaller companies can build AI-native workflows and cross-functional alignment faster.

**Mini case study — MD Anderson vs. Flatiron Health:** MD Anderson dropped IBM Watson for oncology into siloed data, fragmented factions, poor records, and unaligned workflows without asking doctors — it didn't improve care, risked harm, and was stopped. Flatiron Health tackled the **data quality problem first**, cleaned and structured data, then built AI around it — improving outcomes. **AI transformation is a mapping problem.**

### Start with Boring Work
Begin with low-value, annoying work employees hate (forms, expense reports, repetitive approvals, manual reporting, admin). Easy support, immediate benefit — IBM's **"Boring is beautiful."** Quick wins lead employees to suggest more use cases themselves.

### Lesson 2: AI Amplifies the Good and the Bad
AI amplifies what's already in the organization. Ask: What culture do we have? What behaviors are rewarded? If AI doubled those, would it be good or bad? A "move fast and break things" culture becomes dangerous if AI breaks more things faster (Meta shortened it to "move fast"). **Amazon's AI hiring tool** learned gender bias from historical data, penalizing "women's leadership" or "women's soccer captain" — AI didn't invent the bias, it accelerated it. (Amazon pulled back quickly.) Gauthier added: AI can also *detect* pain points by scaling a behavior so leaders see what's wrong — a failed deployment is a learning opportunity if treated as data.

### Lesson 3: Structure Matters
Decide what to **centralize** vs. **decentralize.** Some use cases need governance because risks are high (whether soldiers get fed, whether customers are harmed, legal/safety risk). But full decentralization → everyone uses favorite tools that don't communicate → lots of tinkering, no transformation. Flex the structure: **centralize where risk is high; decentralize where speed, learning, iteration matter; use teams to converge on tools; iterate and learn.** A failed early pilot may mean you're one step closer to the right workflow.

### Lesson 4: Do Not Automate the Soul of the Work
Don't start by automating the most meaningful, identity-rich, craft-based work. **Schiaparelli** (luxury fashion, hand-stitched dresses) — designers' work is the soul; commoditize it and you lose what sells a $100,000 dress. **Grammarly's** controversial feature (edit "as Stephen King" or other real writers) triggered backlash and legal action — writers objected to decades of craft becoming a chatbot feature giving poor advice under their names. There's a difference between using AI to improve a sentence and using AI to impersonate a living expert whose identity and reputation are their livelihood.

### Lesson 5: Leadership Matters
Forcing AI use creates backlash. Employees increasingly disbelieve "AI won't replace you, it will augment you" — they see layoffs citing AI. The more honest message: *"I can't guarantee your job will exist here in five years, but I can guarantee we'll give you tools and training to remain successful in your career."* Two approaches: **Darwinian** (learn AI on your own or move on) vs. **developmental** (company provides training, time, support). Many SV companies lean Darwinian; few provide structured programs. **Accenture** framed AI fluency as critical to serving clients and offered training (though it later had layoffs). **IBM** took a design-thinking approach — asking employees what they needed (linking to "boring is beautiful," starting where employees feel pain).

### Lesson 6: Automation and Productivity
More automatable work → greater productivity gains, but leaders must decide where full automation is safe vs. where human approval/collaboration is needed. Research: **one person + AI** produces strong quality gains; a team + AI is only slightly better — the marginal value of more humans may be smaller than before. As a teams/cognitive-diversity researcher, Caroline found this uncomfortable: diverse teams historically produced better solutions but needed time to exchange ideas; AI now holds knowledge from many domains and can simulate perspectives. Big questions for higher education, expert work, and org design.

### Higher Education and the Democratization of Expertise
When ChatGPT appeared, students used it for recipes and homework; Caroline tested it on a PhD-level Stanford exam and it came close to passing — unsettling after years earning a PhD. Higher education is built on producing and certifying expertise; AI democratizes some forms of expertise, which will disrupt universities.

### Resistance to AI Implementation
Highest resistance often comes from **staff functions — legal, risk, compliance** (trained to ask what could go wrong; necessary voices, include in governance from the start). End users/frontline may resist from distrust or fear of replacement — set realistic expectations. C-level resistance often from CFOs demanding ROI proof (understandable given expensive infrastructure), but cutting AI entirely isn't credible. Customers and external stakeholders (farmers, government, NGOs) have their own change curves.

### Bringing Customers Along
- Large-enterprise customers who aren't early adopters — host internal customer conferences; sometimes customers pay more attention to simpler solutions than AI features.
- Work with advanced adopters as a **tiger team** to test the next value curve. **80-10-10 model:** 10% early adopters, 80% middle, 10% active resistors — don't waste time on resistors; use early adopters to pull the middle forward.
- A company serving 450 small/medium Ukrainian farmers (meteorological data + NDVI for disease/yield prediction): challenge is demographic/cultural (older farmers, low initial trust), but showing the difference vs. traditional methods (disease prevention, crop health) builds trust — focus on **what farmers already care about.**
- A climate-control company: farmers watch the system work on another farm before buying — **peer influence** (farmers trust farmers more than vendors).
- A dairy-sensor company: conservative, skeptical farmers get sensors to test; keep it if they like it, return it if not — **~60% of new clients buy after testing** (lowers risk, lets customers experience value).

### Human Needs Matter More Than the Tech Stack
Customers buy **better outcomes**, not a technology stack — better crops, healthier animals, lower risk, disease prevention, tools that respect their craft. Many SV founders (especially engineers) fall in love with the solution/model/architecture while the customer wants to know what value it creates. Every deployment is an opportunity for feedback and iteration.

### ROI: Cost Cutting vs. New Value Creation
Companies find cost-cutting easier to measure (reduced headcount, faster drafting, lower processing time) than new value (new revenue, products, markets, business models). Most say they want AI to grow revenue, but few have unlocked it — the highest returns come from pointing AI at **revenue generation, not just cost reduction.** Startups have an advantage: they're already creating new value and can design AI strategy around growth. Brynjolfsson's point: society can use AI mainly to cut headcount, or more imaginatively to unlock new products and ways of working.

### Evidence from a Global Virtual Accelerator
An "Inside" study: a 3-month global virtual accelerator with **500 early-stage startups** (all got API credits, technical training, VC pitch opportunities, demo days). The **treatment group** received deeper hands-on support (workflow mapping, business models, financing, AI redesign); the control group got traditional entrepreneurship case discussions. Treatment startups were significantly more likely to acquire paying customers, had **almost twice the revenue**, and were more likely to launch a new product. Early evidence that deeper AI integration improves startup outcomes.

### AI Value Wheel for Startups
Startups use AI across: **Product · Sales & marketing · Operational efficiency · Internal operations.** Internal operations may still be underdeveloped — the more ambitious transformation is making AI part of the company's internal operating system.

### Culture and Psychological Safety
VCs invest in the team that executes with the right culture. **Psychological safety** (Amy Edmondson, Harvard): the belief you won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes — one of the strongest predictors of team success and innovation (Google's own research agreed). It's not comfort or low standards — it's honest, difficult, productive conversations. Edmondson's healthcare research: teams reporting **more** mistakes had **better** outcomes — not because they made more, but because they talked about and learned from them; teams reporting fewer were hiding them. Critical for AI transformation — organizations must surface failures, risks, and unintended consequences quickly.

### High Standards and Psychological Safety
Four quadrants by standards × psychological safety. Most dangerous: **high standards + low psychological safety** (high stakes, people afraid to speak up — dangerous in medicine, war, AI safety). During AI transformation employees must be able to say: this tool failed / this workflow is broken / I'm concerned about cybersecurity / this creates bias / I need help / we're not aligned. Build a **healthy failure culture** (some hold "failure parties").

**Examples:** *Challenger disaster* — an engineer raised reentry concerns but was dismissed by a higher-status superior (low psychological safety + hierarchy). In medicine, nurses may notice mistakes surgeons miss but status prevents speaking up. *Fukushima* — the plant leader realized his plan was failing, gathered the team, admitted it, and asked for help; they redesigned and succeeded with two hours to spare. **Leadership vulnerability can unlock collective intelligence.**

### How Leaders Foster Psychological Safety
Own your mistakes; value every contribution; listen especially to newer employees; share information openly; practice **"no blame, high accountability"**; create forums to discuss failures; invite dissent; distinguish **task conflict** (supports innovation) from **emotional conflict** (freezes teams); keep conflict mission-centered.

### Incentives and AI Transformation
If AI passes expert-level tests, expertise alone may no longer be enough as a reward structure. Incentivize: experimentation, learning, building, cross-functional problem solving, responsible AI use, sharing failures, improving workflows.

### AI as Operating System
Drawing from Y Combinator: treat AI as part of the startup's **operating system**, not merely a tool. An AI-native startup creates a closed system where every decision is documented, meeting notes captured, policies stored, sales/product data centralized — the organization becomes **legible to AI**, and an intelligent layer learns from operations and suggests improvements. This may flatten organizations (middle management historically moved information up/down; AI can encode and distribute it). Emerging archetypes: (1) **everyone is a builder** (HR, sales, ops, engineering all build with AI); (2) **founders own AI-native strategy**; (3) **directly responsible individuals own outcomes.** "Token maxing" may replace headcount as a growth measure — but mostly for early-stage AI-native startups; it can create problems if misused.

### The S-Curve and Staying Ahead
As soon as the first product gains traction, ask what comes next — successful startups die if they cling to the first success and miss the next S-curve (Netflix repeatedly moved through waves). Ukrainian startups should stay ahead of large companies — nimble, constrained, faster.

## Q&A

- **Token maxing, AI brain fry, too much output.** "Token maxing" is mostly for very early AI-native startups; in larger companies it's dysfunctional (if leaders say "use AI or be laid off," employees consume tokens just to prove usage). **"AI brain fry"** — AI dramatically expands the information humans must process; a Berkeley scholar found AI transformation can cause task expansion, massive information flows, and burnout. Create **AI practice spaces** that protect humans and help manage information volume. Example: Palo Alto Networks' small cybersecurity team supervises an army of AI agents defending against hacker AI agents — throwing more people at it doesn't work when volume is too high.
- **Speaking up about problems.** One company's rule: come forward about a problem and it gets fixed; hide it and there are consequences. Caroline starts with the carrot (encourage disclosure/learning) but the stick is also necessary — in high-risk environments, hiding problems can kill people. Links to Apple's **directly responsible individuals** (in the Apple Maps failure, the issue was that the problem hadn't been surfaced properly).
