# Session Summary: Rebecca Hinds
## AI at Work: Organizational Adoption, Human Change, and the Real Conditions for Productivity

> Source: `2026-05-14-Rebecca Hind.pdf` — Berkeley AgriTech Accelerator (Head of the Work AI Institute, Glean)

---

## Core Message
**Individuals get more productive with AI while organizations still fail to improve.** The paradox: **65% of individuals** report productivity gains from AI, but only **6% of organizations** report organizational gains. The reason isn't weak AI — it's that companies underestimate the human, workflow, cultural, and organizational dimensions of adoption.

## Five Principles

### 1. Treat AI as a mindset shift, not just a technology shift
People who see AI as a **teammate / intern / co-worker / second brain** get more value than those who see it as a tool — they iterate, challenge it, test the same prompt across models (it's probabilistic, not deterministic). Framing matters: H&M called it "amplified intelligence," Nordstrom "augmented intelligence." But AI must feel like it's **on people's side**. Don't automate the meaningful parts of work — a Stanford study found 41% of YC AI startups automate tasks people would rather keep human. Some friction creates ownership (**the IKEA effect**); when ideas come too easily, people feel less ownership ("my idea" → "the idea").

### 2. Embed AI in the flow of work — don't bolt it on
Adoption is an **integration** problem, not an access problem. The **toggle tax**: employees switch tools ~**1,200 times/day**, and it takes 23+ minutes to refocus after a switch. **Work slop** (polished but hollow AI output) hits 40% of US workers monthly — often from exhaustion, not laziness. Ask: *for your most important work, how many toggles does it take to use AI?* Agentic AI can compound errors if humans are removed too early.

### 3. Drive change bottom-up, not only top-down
Adoption spreads through people, not memos. **Network effects:** a manager adopting → collaborators 2.4x more likely; a peer adopting → 3.2x. **Cross-functional workers** are the strongest champions (they see handoffs, bottlenecks, silos). Need both: top-down principles for safety (only ~5-10% of orgs have clear AI principles) + bottom-up champions for adoption. Find champions → give access/training → celebrate success. Don't exclude high performers as "too important."

### 4. Beware vanity metrics — measure what matters
Vanity metrics (tool clicks, tokens, prompts, lines of code) create the illusion of adoption. Metrics reward and signal what you value. Measure **efficiency, quality, employee experience, customer experience, learning, collaboration, business outcomes** (e.g. Zapier evaluates efficiency + quality + employee experience). Beware the **tragedy of the commons** — individual productivity (sending work slop, notetaker bots instead of attending) can harm team productivity.

### 5. Redesign roles and org charts for AI
AI can see **across silos**, but humans may lack authority to act across them (retail assortment-planning example — no human owned cross-silo decisions). **HR + IT** are the two most important functions for AI adoption (Moderna merged them). Design physically for collaboration (people 6 ft apart collaborate 4x more than 60 ft apart). Even when AI output is indistinguishable from a CEO's (59% couldn't tell), people rate it lower when they *believe* it's from a bot — human authority and connection still matter.

## Strategic Takeaway
The real question is: **what is AI in service of?** Bob Sutton's test: **remove the word "AI" from the pitch — does the value proposition still hold?** If yes, AI serves a real purpose; if removing it destroys the pitch, you're relying on hype. Fundamentals (data quality, process clarity, governance, human adoption, customer value) don't disappear as models improve — they become **more** important (the **automation paradox**: the more capable a system appears, the less people oversee it).
