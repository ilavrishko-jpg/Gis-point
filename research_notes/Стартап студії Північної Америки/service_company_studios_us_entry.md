## 1. Which IT service / outsourcing / product-development companies created venture studios or product spin-offs, what models did they use, and what were the outcomes?

### Takeaway
The cases with verifiable, durable outcomes are almost all "internal pain turned into a product, funded from service cash flow, then given a dedicated team" (Railsware/Mailtrap, 37signals/Basecamp, MetaLab/Tiny, thoughtbot/FormKeep) rather than formal "agency venture studios" taking equity in outside founders. For the Polish software houses and most US agency-studios named in the brief, I found no public evidence of venture-arm results at all, which is itself a signal.

### Cited Findings

**Railsware (Kyiv; agency turned "product studio") — the strongest CEE precedent**
- Railsware describes itself as a product studio running three products: Mailtrap (email delivery platform), Coupler.io (data integration and analytics) and TitanApps (productivity tools for Jira and monday.com), "serving over six million users" (self-reported) — [Railsware blog](https://railsware.com/blog/how-railsware-built-three-products-from-internal-pains-the-product-studio-model-in-practice/); [Railsware products](https://railsware.com/products/)
- Co-founder Sergiy Korolov: "we started as a service company, but then we built the product expertise and start to deliver our own products" — [SaaS Club podcast #456](https://saasclub.io/podcast/mailtrap-sergiy-korolov-456/)
- Mailtrap origin: in 2011, on a client project, the team accidentally sent 200k test emails to real customers and built an internal tool to prevent it; external demand later turned it into a business (self-reported) — [Railsware handbook, How It All Started](https://railsware.com/handbook/how-it-all-started/)
- Timeline to revenue is long: launched 2011, product-market fit found "with zero marketing", then "another four years to reach $1 million in ARR"; one summary dates $1M ARR to 2020 after 100+ customer interviews used to set pricing. At the time of the podcast: seven-figure ARR, about 40-person team, 100,000+ monthly active users (self-reported) — [SaaS Club podcast #456](https://saasclub.io/podcast/mailtrap-sergiy-korolov-456/)
- Pricing lesson: Mailtrap kept a free tier for casual users and pushed frequent users to paid plans; Railsware says MRR grew 466% in the three years after the change (self-reported) — [Railsware blog, product development dashboard](https://railsware.com/blog/product-development-dashboard/)
- Mailtrap states it has been bootstrapped since 2011 and remains so — [Mailtrap About](https://mailtrap.io/about-us/)
- Coupler.io also came from an internal pain: a spreadsheet formula typo that cost Railsware $120,000 per its blog ($180,000 per a podcast summary — conflicting); it was an internal tool before getting first revenue in spring 2020 — [Railsware handbook](https://railsware.com/handbook/how-it-all-started/); [Failing to Success podcast](https://metacast.app/podcast/failing-to-success/DkArYyT5/25-million-in-software-products-with-railsware-ceo/zZtlRcqV)
- Company-level revenue is unverified and conflicting: a podcast title/summary says about $25M annually from software products with ~200 people; EverybodyWiki lists $20M (2024). No audited figure found — [Failing to Success podcast](https://metacast.app/podcast/failing-to-success/DkArYyT5/25-million-in-software-products-with-railsware-ceo/zZtlRcqV); [EverybodyWiki, Mailtrap](https://en.everybodywiki.com/Mailtrap)
- Railsware published a first-person DOU column on building own products inside a service company ("our path from zero to $1M ARR") — the most relevant candid account, but I could not open it — [DOU column](https://dou.ua/lenta/columns/how-to-make-products-in-service-company/)

**37signals / Basecamp (US; the canonical agency-to-product precedent)**
- Founded 1999 as a web design firm; released Basecamp in 2004; once Basecamp revenue passed web-design revenue in 2005 the transition was complete — [Wikipedia, 37signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37signals)
- Their own description: the move from clients to products was made "one day at a time" (gradual, client work funded the product) — [Signal v. Noise](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/650-ask-37signals-how-to-go-from-clients-to-products)

- Divante spun its open-source Vue Storefront project out into a separate startup in 2020 (surfaced in a search summary about Polish software consultancies; page not opened) — [Medium, Poland's Software Consultants](https://medium.com/@QuantumDom/polands-software-consultants-a-short-history-of-the-backbone-of-the-polish-tech-economy-53d0a2ccc377)

**Ukraine (outsourcers with ecosystems rather than studios)**

**US agency-studios**
- Sidebench (Los Angeles) positions itself as both a corporate innovation lab and a startup venture studio that invests in select early-stage B2B startups, often alongside corporate clients; no equity terms published — [Crunchbase, Sidebench](https://www.crunchbase.com/organization/sidebench-studios); [AdForum profile](https://www.adforum.com/agency/6682964/profile/sidebench)

### Inferences
- The pattern that repeatedly works for a service company: (a) product originates in a pain the agency itself had, (b) service revenue pays for a long, slow validation period (Mailtrap: roughly nine years from first build to $1M ARR), (c) the product gets a dedicated team and its own P&L once it shows pull, (d) the company prunes rather than accumulates products (37signals 2014; thoughtbot selling FormKeep, retiring Upcase).
- thoughtbot's FormKeep sale is the cleanest statement of the core structural problem: a growing product needs a dedicated team, and a consultancy that will not provide one should sell or spin it out.
- The absence of public results from Polish software houses' and US dev agencies' "venture" arms suggests those arms are mostly marketing/positioning or small sweat-equity side bets, not a proven profit line. Treat any agency's "venture studio" page as a claim until numbers are shown.

These need direct site reads; they were not reachable in this session.

## 2. How do the Genesis / SKELAR-style "co-founding company / venture builder" models from Ukraine work, and what are the results?

### Takeaway
These are not agency side-projects but capital-backed company factories: the builder funds the business, supplies central functions (legal, finance, recruiting, marketing expertise), recruits an entrepreneur-CEO, and spins out winners; their core competence is paid user acquisition for consumer subscription products sold largely to US consumers. Neither Genesis nor SKELAR publishes equity terms, and in June 2026 the US FTC sued Genesis over its subscription practices, which is now the main cautionary data point for the whole model.

### Cited Findings

**Genesis**
- Genesis calls itself a "co-founding company" building global tech businesses with CEE entrepreneurs; founded 2008 by Vladimir Mnogoletniy and Vasiliy Ulyanov — [gen.tech](https://gen.tech/en); [BusinessABC, Genesis](https://businessabc.net/wiki/genesis)
- Operating model as described in the CEO's profile: Genesis co-founds companies rather than investing in them, carries legal, finance and recruiting centrally, and spins out the winners; around 3,000 people across 25+ companies (profile data, self-reported origin) — [fndrlist, Volodymyr Mnoholietnii](https://fndrlist.com/startup-ecosystem/volodymyr-mnoholietnii)
- Spun-out companies: Jiji, BetterMe and Headway were incubated inside Genesis and later became independent; active ecosystem projects named by Genesis include OBRIO, Universe, AMO and Boosters — [BusinessABC, Genesis](https://businessabc.net/wiki/genesis); [Lviv IT Cluster](https://itcluster.lviv.ua/en/genesis-expands-its-product-ecosystem-new-office-opening-in-lviv/)
- Jiji: co-founded within Genesis by CEO Anton Volianskyi and Vladimir Mnogoletniy; independent since 2019; has raised nearly $55M (Knuru Capital, TA Ventures, Horizon Capital); valuation reported as "several hundred million dollars" with a stated billion-dollar target; bought Bangladesh's largest marketplace Bikroy (reported 2026). Note Jiji targets Africa/Asia, not North America — [dev.ua](https://dev.ua/en/news/genesis-1778644661); [Wikipedia, Jiji Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiji_Africa)
- BetterMe: founded 2016 in Kyiv by Victoria Repa inside Genesis; third-party revenue estimates for 2025 cluster around $75–80M (RocketReach $75.8M, ZoomInfo $80M) with an outlier at $22M — all unaudited estimates — [RocketReach](https://rocketreach.co/answers/what-is-the-annual-revenue-of-betterme_b453fcd9fc8492e5); [ZoomInfo](https://www.zoominfo.com/c/betterme-international-ltd/478148813); [Reviewbolt](https://reviewbolt.com/r/betterme.world)
- Headway (book-summary/micro-learning app): third-party estimates of $160M revenue in 2025 (GetLatka, which also shows a "$1B valuation") and $126.9M (ZoomInfo); unaudited, and aggregator pages for "Headway" sometimes confuse it with unrelated companies of the same name — [GetLatka](https://getlatka.com/companies/headway.co#customers); [ZoomInfo](https://www.zoominfo.com/c/headway/482374136)
- Platform risk, 2019: Apple removed Genesis apps from the App Store; the company called it normal — [AIN, Dec 2019](https://en.ain.ua/2019/12/17/apple-removes-apps-by-genesis/)
- Regulatory risk, 2026: the FTC sued the "Genesis Tech enterprise" (press release 17 June 2026) for unlawful subscription schemes; a federal court temporarily halted the schemes pending trial; no penalty or settlement yet and the allegations are unproven — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-sues-stop-sprawling-enterprise-operating-unlawful-subscription-schemes)
- Details of the FTC case: 15 corporations and eight individuals named, including the two founders; apps named include Wisey, PDF Guru/PDF Master, Lumi, Nebula, MadMuscles/Harna/Unimeal; the structure is described as subsidiaries incorporated in Cyprus (and Delaware shells) operating from Kyiv and marketing to US consumers; the FTC alleges nearly $250M in global revenue from early 2023 to mid-2025 for the network — [Kyiv Independent](https://kyivindependent.com/us-trade-regulator-accuses-billion-dollar-ukrainian-it-giant-of-fraud/); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/ftc-genesis-tech-subscription-schemes); [dev.ua](https://dev.ua/en/news/genesis-1781753702); [FTC complaint PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Growthmind-Wisey-Complaint.pdf)
- The FTC complaint lists Universe Group as part of Genesis's active portfolio and as a Cyprus limited company with its principal place of business in Kyiv; BetterMe is not among the apps named in the case — [FTC complaint PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Growthmind-Wisey-Complaint.pdf)
- Law-firm commentary treats the complaint as a template for US subscription enforcement (ROSCA: disclosure, consent, simple cancellation) rather than a one-off — [Sigma Law Group](https://sigmalawgroup.com/blog/2026-07-07-ftc-genesis-subscription/); [Adventures in Law](https://www.adventures-in-law.com/blogs/ftcs-genesis-tech-complaint-signals-more-than-another-rosca-case/)

**SKELAR**
- Self-description: a venture builder that creates international tech businesses by the principles of venture investing; current site wording says it backs consumer tech companies "with capital, platform, and experience" — [skelar.tech](https://www.skelar.tech/)
- Operating since 2015 per Ukrainian press (PitchBook also says 2015; other aggregators say 2014); a past press figure of 800 employees; about a dozen businesses "from EdTech to marketplaces" (self-reported) — [The Page](https://thepage.ua/ua/news/yaki-veteranski-startapi-berut-uchast-u-skelar-veteran-venture-program); [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/profiles/investor/527524-57)
- Known portfolio names: Brighterly (math and reading platform for kids), PawChamp, Dressly, RiseGuide, Doggy Plan, Relatio — consumer niches (EdTech, petcare, fashion tech, mental health) — [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/profiles/investor/527524-57); [The Page](https://thepage.ua/ua/news/yaki-veteranski-startapi-berut-uchast-u-skelar-veteran-venture-program)
- SKELAR also runs an equity-free Veteran Venture Program (grants reported as up to $20,000; one source says top projects got $44,000) — a talent funnel, not its core deal — [dev.ua](https://dev.ua/news/hranty-dlia-veteraniv-z-it-dosvidom-1777900104)

**Newer Ukrainian builders and the "co-founding" trend**
- Spalah (launched Aug 2024 by Ukrainian IT entrepreneurs incl. Ivan Guzenko): tickets from $100,000 up to $500,000 per project; each project pitches an internal investment board for first-year funding; focus on adtech/martech, apps and publishing, AI; supports from pre-seed to round A with hiring and legal; claims 10+ businesses already built in European and US markets — [AIN, Aug 2024](https://en.ain.ua/2024/08/07/ukrainian-it-entrepreneurs-have-launched-the-spalah-venture-builder/); [spalah.tech](https://spalah.tech/en)
- CLUST (Ukrainian venture builder) states the logic openly: it takes the majority stake when it does most of the early work, and its share shrinks when the founder brings most of the groundwork — [Under30CEO](https://www.under30ceo.com/venture-builder/)
- Ukrainian business press describes co-founding as "a substitute for early-stage investment" and a new growth model for Ukrainian IT, with Genesis as the most visible beneficiary — [InVenture](https://inventure.com.ua/en/news/ukraine/ukrainian-it-is-searching-for-a-new-growth-model:-cofounding-is-becoming-a-substitute-for-early-stage-investment); [Ekonomichna Pravda](https://epravda.com.ua/tehnologiji/kofaunding-noviy-drayver-zrostannya-dlya-ukrajinskogo-it-rinku-819553/)

### Inferences
- The Ukrainian builders' edge is not engineering but a repeatable performance-marketing and subscription-monetisation machine pointed at Tier-1 (mainly US) consumers, plus central back-office. A service company's bench does not supply that edge; this is the key difference between "co-founding companies" and agency studios.
- Since builders fund everything and recruit the CEO, the builder is almost certainly the majority holder at start (consistent with CLUST's stated logic and with general studio norms in section 3), with CEO equity/options earned over time. This is inference: no Genesis or SKELAR term was found.
- The FTC case changes the risk calculus for anyone copying the playbook in the US: aggressive subscription funnels, hard cancellation and multi-entity structures are now an explicit enforcement target, with founders named personally.
- Typical legal wrapper in this ecosystem appears to be Cyprus holding/operating companies with Kyiv teams and sometimes Delaware entities (per the FTC complaint) — different from the Delaware C-corp parent that US VCs expect.

### Gaps
- Equity splits between Genesis/SKELAR and founder-CEOs, vesting, buy-out and spin-out terms: not published; multiple searches in English and Ukrainian found nothing reliable. One low-quality site claimed Genesis holds about 40–45% of Jiji with no source — not used.
- Audited revenues for Genesis, SKELAR, BetterMe, Headway, Universe Group, Brighterly: none found; only aggregator estimates.
- The connection of Preply to Genesis/SKELAR mentioned in the brief could not be confirmed by any source found (no source linked Preply to either builder).
- Outcome of the FTC case after the temporary order: nothing found beyond June–July 2026 coverage.
- The epravda and InVenture co-founding articles could not be opened; they likely contain the best ecosystem numbers.

## 3. Business models for a service company moving into products: structures, typical equity percentages and deal terms, and the known pitfalls

### Takeaway
Dedicated venture studios take large stakes (GSSN data, via secondary sources: 15–80% at formation, about 34% on average) because they supply idea, team and capital; an agency contributing only build work is in a much weaker position and practitioner sources consistently say pure equity-for-build deals fail unless cash covers cost and scope is fixed. No reliable benchmark for "agency cash + equity" percentages was found.

### Cited Findings

**Model types and equity ranges**
- GSSN data as relayed by secondary sources: studios hold 15% to 80% of initial equity, averaging around 34% at formation — [Conviction, The Rise of Venture Studios](https://convictionlog.substack.com/p/the-rise-of-venture-studios); [MIT thesis, Venture Studios (2021)](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/139445/munoz-nmunoz-sm-idm-2021-thesis.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)
- Studios justify 40–50%+ by their higher operating cost; they often take common/founder shares for the "sweat" and then invest cash for preferred shares — [Focused Chaos, Venture Studio Math](https://www.focusedchaos.co/p/venture-studio-math)
- Who did the early work drives the split: studio brings in a founder after doing the groundwork = larger studio share; founder arrives with own idea and work = smaller share — [Focused Chaos, The Many Flavors of Venture Studios](https://www.focusedchaos.co/p/the-many-flavors-of-venture-studios)
- One 2026 overview describes two shapes: single-entity studio at 40–50%, and a hybrid with a smaller stake (20–35%) plus ongoing fees and a revenue share (vendor blog) — [Padiso](https://www.padiso.co/blog/venture-studio-equity-structures-2026/)
- Big Venture Studio Research 2024 (industry-sponsored, PhD-led) reports that lower studio equity stakes may correlate with higher venture success rates within the ranges studied — [Inniches](https://inniches.com/big-venture-studio-research)
- GSSN numbers are criticised for selection and survivorship bias — [MIT thesis](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/139445/munoz-nmunoz-sm-idm-2021-thesis.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)
- Other deal terms that come with studio equity: board seats, approval rights and restrictions — [Focused Chaos](https://www.focusedchaos.co/p/the-many-flavors-of-venture-studios)
- Founder-side pushback (2026): a studio taking more than about 20% at idea stage can make the company hard to fund with VCs; critics describe some studios as "dev shops that accept equity instead of cash"; a studio living mainly on retainers behaves like an agency that also writes cheques, one living on equity outcomes behaves like a fund — [Discury teardown](https://discury.io/digest/teardown-venture-studio-service-fees-and-value-proposition-d253); [Discury teardown 2](https://digest.discury.io/teardown-venture-studio-equity-demands-3942)
- Studios commonly cover burn with corporate partnerships, sidecar funds and services — [Highline Beta](https://www.highlinebeta.com/blog/launching-a-venture-studio-the-right-way-our-comprehensive-checklist)
- Co-founding/venture-builder variant with cash tickets: Spalah $100k–$500k per project with investment-board approval — [AIN](https://en.ain.ua/2024/08/07/ukrainian-it-entrepreneurs-have-launched-the-spalah-venture-builder/)

**Pitfalls and post-mortems**
- Agency guide (2026): the equity-for-build deals that failed "had one thing in common: the agency treated the equity as the primary compensation and the project as a side bet"; emotionally invested teams kept saying yes to extra scope; sloppy paperwork turned a nominal "15% of a unicorn" into 0.4% after four rounds — [72Technologies](https://www.72technologies.com/blog/equity-for-build-deals-agency-cap-table)
- Core flaw of sweat-equity build deals: "no scope, no boundaries, no completion criteria" — [Medium / The Startup](https://medium.com/swlh/hiring-a-developer-for-your-startup-part-i-sweat-equity-free-development-937811fc6eb6)
- Legal case study: no vesting or reverse-vesting meant unearned shares could not be reclaimed — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/shareholders/1661772/sweat-equity-gone-wrong-legal-takeaways-from-a-startups-early-miscalculation)
- Running agency and studio in parallel: a former studio team says doing both at once "was a mistake that cost us a great deal of time and emotional strain" — [Joshua Tiong, Venture Studio – Attempt 1 (2018)](https://joshuationg.squarespace.com/blog/2018/12/venture-studio-attempt-1)
- Attention problem confirmed from the agency side: thoughtbot sold FormKeep because it needed a dedicated team the consultancy was not providing — [thoughtbot](https://thoughtbot.com/blog/formkeep-moves-to-furious-collective)
- Portfolio sprawl: 37signals cut to one product in 2014 because many products with a small team left it "scattered" — [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/benkepes/2014/02/05/37signals-no-more-changes-name-to-basecamp-and-drops-all-products-but-its-namesake/)
- IP hygiene for spin-offs: without a transfer or licence agreement from the software house, the new company does not own what it sells — [Mizzox](https://mizzox.com/baza-wiedzy/czym-jest-spin-off/)
- Market-standard founder vesting cited by a Ukrainian law firm: four years with a one-year cliff — [Barbashyn Law Firm](https://barbashyn.law/en/statti/zaluchennya-investyczij-v-it-yurydychni-aspekty-dlya-startapiv/)

### Inferences
- Five workable structures, ranked by evidence of success for service companies: (1) internal product from own pain, funded by service cash flow, later separate team/P&L (Railsware, 37signals); (2) agency cash flow used to acquire small products (MetaLab to Tiny); (3) capital-backed co-founding with a recruited CEO (Genesis, SKELAR, Spalah) — needs real capital and a distribution engine; (4) cash + equity hybrid with outside founders — viable only if cash covers delivery cost; (5) pure services-for-equity — the most commonly reported failure.
- A sensible rule derived from the sources: price sweat equity so that cash covers at least cost, fix scope and completion criteria, vest the agency's equity against milestones, and keep the total agency stake low enough (well under the ~20% red-flag level for idea-stage outside founders) to leave the company fundable — unless the agency is truly the originator and capital provider, in which case studio-level stakes (30%+) are the norm.
- "Bench-driven products" (building because engineers are idle) is not documented as such in sources found, but it is the mirror image of the documented success pattern (product pulled by a real, self-experienced pain and validated with customers before scaling).

### Gaps
- No reliable data on typical agency cash-discount-for-equity terms (e.g. "30–50% rate discount for 5–15% equity"); searches returned only anecdotes. Mark any such numbers in the final report as practitioner lore, not benchmark.
- GSSN and Big Venture Studio Research primary reports were not read; figures here are second-hand.
- No quantified failure rates for agency-run studios specifically.
- The Focused Chaos "Many Flavors" article (which has an agency-studio flavour section) could not be opened.

## 4. What is practically needed to launch into the US market from abroad (entity, banking, IP, compliance, payments, budget, channels, fundraising)?

### Takeaway
The entity/banking layer is cheap and remote-friendly (Delaware C-corp via Stripe Atlas for about $500, Mercury account without SSN), while the expensive parts are the flip if you start with a foreign company ($15k–$80k+), compliance for B2B sales (SOC 2: roughly $25k–$130k in year one) and above all go-to-market, where a first US sales hire alone runs about $300k OTE. For consumer subscription products, US consumer-protection enforcement (ROSCA/FTC) is now a first-order risk, per the 2026 Genesis case.

### Cited Findings

**Entity formation**
- Stripe Atlas: $500 one-time fee covering Delaware filing and first-year registered agent, then $100/year for the agent; includes EIN (filed without SSN per one comparison), founder equity issuance, 83(b) election filing, legal templates, Stripe credits; forms a Delaware C-corp or LLC; non-US founders are a stated target market. Delaware annual taxes are extra (review sites, 2026) — [Rho review](https://www.rho.co/blog/stripe-atlas-review); [Sparklaunch comparison](https://sparklaun.ch/compare/stripe-atlas); [StartupSavant review](https://startupsavant.com/service-reviews/stripe-atlas)
- For non-residents the only Delaware requirement is a registered agent with a Delaware address; a non-resident-owned corporation is taxed as a C-corp and dividends to non-resident shareholders face withholding; investors are the usual reason to choose a C-corp anyway — [Terms.law guide](https://terms.law/INC/DE-inc/delaware-corp-nonresidents.html)

**Banking**
- Mercury opens accounts for non-residents without SSN or US address, given a US entity (LLC or C-corp) and EIN; passport accepted; founders from countries on Mercury's prohibited list are rejected; reports of tighter screening of "thin" business profiles in 2026 (a real website and clear business description help); banking is called the biggest practical hurdle for international founders (vendor/SEO sources) — [Plane](https://plane.com/blog/international-founders-us-bank-accounts); [Doola](https://www.doola.com/university/how-to-open-an-account-with-mercury/); [Awisee](https://awisee.com/blog/us-business-bank-accounts-for-non-residents/)

**Flip structures and IP**
- A flip is a share-for-share exchange: new Delaware parent, foreign shareholders swap into Delaware stock, the foreign company becomes a wholly owned subsidiary (or is dissolved) — [Fellow.legal](https://www.fellow.legal/blog/us-flips-for-international-founders); [Story LLP](https://story.law/knowledge/delaware-flip)
- IP is the key issue: investors expect code and trademarks assigned up to the Delaware parent; an alternative is leaving IP in the subsidiary under an intercompany licence, which may not satisfy US VCs; in some jurisdictions the assignment is a taxable disposal at market value, so IP valuation is the costly part — [ZeccaRoss Law](https://www.zeccarosslaw.com/areas-served/delaware-flip-for-european-founders-costs-timing-and-legal-tradeoffs-144b3); [Turing VC manual](https://turingventurecapital.com/manual/chapter/delaware-c-corp)
- Flip cost estimates vary: $15k–$40k (US + home counsel + tax), $25k–$80k, $10k–$14.5k for US-side work only, or $30k–$120k depending on origin jurisdiction (law-firm marketing pages) — [ZeccaRoss Law](https://www.zeccarosslaw.com/areas-served/delaware-flip-for-european-founders-costs-timing-and-legal-tradeoffs-144b3); [Causo guide](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/non-us-founder-us-seed)
- Conflicting views on timing (2026): one guide says the flip is no longer a prerequisite for a US seed and that top funds have wired into UK, Canadian, German, Israeli and Singaporean entities with a side-letter to flip at Series A; another says most US VCs will "signal-not-deal" until the flip closes. Neither names Ukrainian or Polish entities as accepted — [Causo guide](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/non-us-founder-us-seed); [Fellow.legal](https://www.fellow.legal/blog/us-flips-for-international-founders)

**Compliance**
- SOC 2 (2026 estimates, mostly vendor blogs): automation platforms about $7.5k–$15k/year at startup tier (AWS Marketplace list: Vanta Essentials $14,000/yr for 1–20 employees; Drata Foundation $15,000/yr for 1–50); auditor $8k–$25k for Type I and $15k–$40k for Type II (partner auditors from about $2.5k); all-in year one from about $25k–$50k lean to $60k–$130k including internal time; Type I in roughly 3–4 months, Type II 9–18 months from start; renewal quotes reported to jump 40–100% on tier changes — [Patotski](https://patotski.com/blog/soc-2-cost-for-startup-saas/); [Cavanex](https://cavanex.com/blog/soc-2-compliance-cost-2026); [SOC2 Auditors](https://soc2auditors.org/insights/best-soc-2-software-startups/)
- US sales tax: a foreign business with no US office or staff has no physical nexus but can still trigger economic nexus per state (commonly about $100,000 of sales into the state, some states add a transaction count) — [Neubase](https://neubase.co/blog/sales-tax-software-saas/); [Jumpstart Partners](https://jumpstartpartners.finance/blog/saas-sales-tax-economic-nexus-compliance-guide)
- Subscription/consumer law: the FTC's June 2026 Genesis Tech action is being read by US lawyers as a field guide to subscription enforcement (clear disclosure, informed consent, easy cancellation) — [Sigma Law Group](https://sigmalawgroup.com/blog/2026-07-07-ftc-genesis-subscription/)

**Payments and pricing**
- Merchant-of-record route: Paddle calculates, collects and remits sales tax/VAT and says the seller has no sales-tax liability for Paddle transactions; commonly cited fee 5% + $0.50 per transaction; caveats: revenue sold outside Paddle (direct invoices) still counts toward state thresholds, and at about $500k+ MRR the fee can exceed the cost of doing tax in-house — [Paddle](https://www.paddle.com/blog/sales-tax-subscriptions); [Commenda](https://www.commenda.io/integrations/paddle/sales-tax-and-global-vat-software-for-paddle); [Causo, Stripe vs Paddle](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/stripe-vs-paddle-saas-payments-2026)

**Go-to-market budgets and people**
- First US sales hire: about $150k base + $150k on-target earnings; first-year US team building $200k–$500k (accelerator estimate) — [M Accelerator](https://maccelerator.la/en/blog/growth-strategy/how-to-launch-a-b2b-saas-in-the-us-as-a-foreign-founder/)
- A US-expansion advisory firm puts a full Year-1 US entry at $1.5M–$3M plus a 25–30% contingency; a wrong first hire costs $150k–$200k in compensation plus 6–12 months lost (advisory marketing) — [US Expansion Partners](https://www.usxp.co/resources/are-you-ready-to-enter-the-us-market-most-european-founders-arent/)
- Balderton's list of European founders' US mistakes includes underestimating sales and marketing (especially product-led founders), and both hiring too cheaply and hiring big-company executives who relied on resources a startup lacks — [Balderton](https://www.balderton.com/resources/the-top-5-mistakes-in-us-expansion-mistake-4-underestimating-sales-and-marketing/)
- Common ordering mistake: testing the US with a single account executive instead of a leader; founders budget for the hire and forget payroll, compliance and legal around it — [Captivate Talent](https://www.captivatetalent.com/blog/saas-startup-expansion-how-to-hire-in-the-us-market); [Tech Funding News](https://techfundingnews.com/the-first-us-hire-why-european-founders-keep-getting-the-order-wrong/)

**Channels for remote-first teams**
- Product-led/organic can work fully remotely: Mailtrap found product-market fit "with zero marketing" and reached 100k+ monthly active users from Kyiv before formal marketing — [SaaS Club podcast #456](https://saasclub.io/podcast/mailtrap-sergiy-korolov-456/)
- Paid acquisition into app stores/web funnels is the Ukrainian builders' main US channel (Genesis ecosystem apps marketed to US consumers; nearly $250M alleged revenue 2023–mid-2025 for five companies) — and carries App Store and FTC risk — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-sues-stop-sprawling-enterprise-operating-unlawful-subscription-schemes); [AIN 2019](https://en.ain.ua/2019/12/17/apple-removes-apps-by-genesis/)
- AppSumo: revenue share is unpublished; third-party estimates say AppSumo keeps roughly 30–70% depending on deal type; best-case example Frase (2021): nearly $800k in 28 days and 8,000 customers; downsides: lifetime-deal buyers generate heavy support and feature demands, with founders reporting the launch "consumed their roadmap for three to six months" — [LaunchBuff](https://launchbuff.com/blog/is-appsumo-worth-it-saas-founders-2026); [Startup GTM](https://startupgtm.substack.com/p/appsumo-100mn-playbook-strategies); [AppSumo](https://appsumo.com/blog/partner-promotion)

### Inferences
- For a new product (no legacy foreign entity holding the IP), incorporating the Delaware C-corp first and having the service company assign/contribute IP from day one avoids the flip cost and the taxable IP transfer entirely. The service company can then hold its stake as a shareholder and bill the C-corp for development under an intercompany agreement.
- Budget tiers implied by the sources: (a) self-serve/PLG SaaS or app sold via merchant of record — entity, bank, MoR and basic legal for low thousands of dollars, with marketing as the real variable; (b) B2B mid-market requiring SOC 2 and a US seller — realistically $300k–$500k+ in year one; (c) full enterprise US entry — $1.5M+.
- A merchant of record (Paddle-type) is the lowest-overhead way for a foreign-run entity to handle US state sales tax at the start; revisit at scale.
- Service companies are structurally strong at build and weak at exactly what the sources say foreign founders underestimate: US sales and marketing.

### Gaps
- Not verified against primary pages (Stripe, Mercury, IRS, Delaware): exact Atlas inclusions, Mercury's current country restrictions (in particular for Ukraine-resident founders), Form 5472/1120 filing duties for foreign-owned US corporations, Delaware franchise tax calculation. The 2025 BOI-reporting exemption for US entities appeared only as a banner in a search result.

- No data found on Product Hunt, outbound, or marketplace (Atlassian, Shopify, monday.com) channel economics, although Railsware's TitanApps shows the marketplace route in practice.
- No data found on fundraising success rates of CEE-founded companies with US investors, or on Ukraine/Poland-specific tax treatment of flips and IP assignment.
- Canada-specific entry requirements were not researched; all findings concern the US.
- Most figures in this section are from vendor or law-firm marketing content and should be presented as indicative ranges.

## 5. Which bootstrapped / micro-studio / portfolio-of-small-products models are relevant, and what are their economics?

### Takeaway
The best-documented path from agency to portfolio is MetaLab to Tiny: agency cash flow used to buy and hold profitable small internet businesses, now a public company with $203.8M revenue and $37.9M adjusted EBITDA (FY2025; currency as stated in the release, not confirmed here — Tiny is TSX-listed, so probably Canadian dollars) — but still majority non-recurring revenue. Railsware is the closest CEE analogue on the "build" side; SureSwift is the "buy small SaaS" analogue; Late Checkout is the audience-led version with unaudited numbers.

### Cited Findings
- Tiny (TSX: TINY): FY2025 revenue $203.8M, up 5% (12% excluding divested entities); adjusted EBITDA $37.9M, up 22%; recurring revenue $57.8M, up 50% (driven by the Serato acquisition), equal to 28% of total revenue versus 20% in FY2024 — [Tiny FY2025 results release](https://www.newsfilecorp.com/release/290377/Tiny-Reports-Q4-2025-and-Full-Year-2025-Results)
- Tiny FY2025 free cash flow $25.3M — [Tiny Q4/FY2025 earnings presentation](http://s202.q4cdn.com/676416790/files/doc_financials/2025/q4/Tiny-Ltd-Q4-and-Fiscal-2025-Earnings-Call-Presentation-vF.pdf)
- Tiny's origin: MetaLab agency cash flow funded acquisitions of small profitable software businesses; MetaLab became a Tiny subsidiary in Jan 2017; Tiny listed on the TSX in 2023; one profile cites 32+ businesses and 900+ employees — [Never Enough, What is Tiny](https://www.neverenough.com/what-is-tiny); [Draft wiki: Tiny](https://wiki.riteme.site/wiki/Draft:Tiny_(holding_company))
- A secondary case study attributes part of Tiny's economics to Canadian advantages: R&D tax credits of 15–40%, lower salaries, public healthcare — [First MRR case study](https://first-mrr.com/study/tiny-capital)
- SureSwift Capital: buys SaaS businesses from independent founders; deal counts range from "more than 45" (Hahnbeck) to "50+" (own LinkedIn); team of 150+; two investor-backed funds, with a Fund C launched in 2024 — [Hahnbeck](https://www.hahnbeck.com/blog/saas-acquirers-sureswift-capital-shopcircle-tiny-wecommerce-relay-pantastic-threecolts-more); [SureSwift LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/company/sureswiftcapital)
- Late Checkout (Greg Isenberg): holding company with an agency (LCA: design/product/AI workflow projects and retainers), a studio that builds or acquires products, and a fund; includes a nested Boring holding (BoringMarketing AI-assisted SEO, BoringAds); described as an eight-figure-revenue, mid-seven-figure-profit bootstrapped group across six businesses — figures are from interviews and third-party modelling, not disclosed financials — [Capitaly, How Greg Isenberg Makes Money](https://www.capitaly.vc/blog/how-greg-isenberg-makes-money-late-checkout-exits-advisory-media-and-angel-bets); [Capitaly, productized services to venture studio](https://www.capitaly.vc/blog/greg-isenberg-productized-services-to-venture-studio); [Starter Story summary](https://stationlm.com/analysis/100fa8b7-ee89-4d94-bf34-0401cdc04d4f)
- Railsware as a bootstrapped multi-product studio: three products, six million users (self-reported), Mailtrap bootstrapped since 2011; company revenue estimates $20M–$25M (unverified) — [Railsware blog](https://railsware.com/blog/how-railsware-built-three-products-from-internal-pains-the-product-studio-model-in-practice/); [Mailtrap About](https://mailtrap.io/about-us/); [Failing to Success podcast](https://metacast.app/podcast/failing-to-success/DkArYyT5/25-million-in-software-products-with-railsware-ceo/zZtlRcqV)

### Inferences
- Three distinct portfolio logics a service company can copy: build from own pain (Railsware), buy small profitable products with service cash flow (Tiny, SureSwift), or build distribution/audience first and attach agency plus products to it (Late Checkout). The second and third do not depend on the bench having spare capacity.
- Even Tiny, after roughly a decade, earns only 28% of revenue as recurring; agencies and services still carry the group. A service company should expect the service arm to remain the cash engine for many years.
- Late Checkout's "agency + studio + fund under one holdco with a media front end" is the closest Western equivalent to an agency-to-studio model that claims to work, but its numbers are unaudited and founder-brand dependent.

- SureSwift fund sizes, returns and purchase multiples: not found.
- Tiny's debt level and segment-level margins: not captured (available in its MD&A).
- Late Checkout financials are not independently verifiable.
